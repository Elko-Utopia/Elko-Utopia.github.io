The Star of Embarkation: Planet and Art

    1. Map requirements and Photoshop sketches

    I wanted to make a planetary map that would support the story of The Star of Embarkation while still making geographic sense.

    The story begins when humans arrive from Earth and come into conflict with the planet’s native intelligent species, the Sylphs. That conflict eventually becomes a war between the two civilizations. After the war, both societies develop their own pro-war and anti-war organizations. The map therefore needs geographic buffers between the different factions, as well as rivers to supply human towns. I also wanted steep mountains, deserts, forests, lakes, and wetlands, so that places such as the mountain refuges in the story would have a plausible setting.

    The story does not involve naval warfare. Several major continents would also have a substantial influence on a Sylph civilization that developed on this planet, and on the direction of the story. I therefore decided on a single main continent. Whichever civilization held it would have an overwhelming geographic advantage, giving both sides a lasting reason to fight over it.

    In my setting, the Sylph civilization endured extremely harsh natural conditions in the distant past. To reflect that history, I planned an enormous impact crater that would give the continent an overall crescent shape.

    While sketching in Photoshop, I felt that a planet with only one main landmass looked unconvincing, so I added outlying archipelagos. Since the formation of many islands is associated with tectonic activity, I also drew plate boundaries into the sketch. They helped me place the islands and gave me a basis for designing the terrain.

    From a flat sketch to a sphere

    After finishing the sketch, I imported it into GPlates to examine the continent’s shape and position on a globe. The map projection had changed the apparent proportions, outlines, and relative positions of the land compared with the flat drawing.

    Using the Photoshop sketch as a reference, I redrew the coastlines and plate boundaries as vectors in GPlates. This gave me a simplified outline that worked with the actual shape of the sphere.

    2. Drawing continents and plates in GPlates

    I set the planet’s diameter to 12,050 km: slightly smaller than Earth, but still on a terrestrial planet’s scale. Together with gravity somewhat lower than Earth’s, this gives the characters more freedom of movement. The shorter geographic distances also make it easier to arrange journeys between regions. The main continent covers approximately 36.01 million square kilometers.

    Naming the geographic regions

    Humans bring memories of their old world to the new continent. The Sylphs name places after water sources, landforms, and their faith in the Spirit Tree. The main continent and mountain range therefore have two sets of names. Plate names primarily identify their positions on the globe; the tables below give every plate’s name and location.

    Once I had established the coasts, islands, and plates, the next task was to turn those geological structures into terrain with actual changes in elevation. GPlates works well for spherical coastlines and plates, but I needed to assign heights point by point along the ridges, so I moved to QGIS to build the initial contoured terrain.

    3. Building the initial contoured terrain in QGIS

    With the continents drawn, I began deciding where the mountains would extend, where they would rise, and how they would meet the lowlands. Drawing every contour by hand would have taken a great deal of work. It was also difficult to judge in advance where each elevation boundary should fall along a long, narrow mountain range.

    Instead, I drew ridge control lines. A polyline established the direction of the range, and I placed nodes with specified elevations along it. I only had to decide where the mountains should go and how high those points should be. The software could then estimate the ground between the ridge nodes and the zero-elevation coastline. The control lines defined the ridge positions and heights; an initial interpolation turned those separate constraints into continuous terrain.

    Trying different smoothing algorithms

    When drawing the ridge controls, I used the plate relationships I had designed earlier. Two plates collide on the western side of New Land, producing the planet’s largest and longest mountain range: the New Andes. The Andes are among Earth’s longest continental mountain ranges. Reusing a familiar name for a similar landform in a new world was part of the immigrant culture I wanted to show.

    I wanted this mountain barrier to intercept moist ocean winds moving from west to east and limit the amount of moisture reaching the continental interior. Rain would fall on the windward slopes, leaving the land behind the mountains drier and creating a desert in the center of the continent. That desert could also separate the main human and Sylph territories in the story. I would need climate simulations to find out whether this arrangement actually worked.

    I first used natural-neighbor interpolation to connect the ridge nodes and zero-elevation coast into continuous initial terrain. The image on the left shows the elevation controls; the one on the right shows the resulting surface. The comparison makes it easier to see how the ridge positions shape the slopes.

    The initial terrain still had slopes that looked too abrupt. I tried TIN interpolation, which preserves the control points but creates obvious triangular facets on the hillsides. GRASS GIS regularized splines produced a smoother surface, but the seams between processing regions were just as conspicuous.

    I eventually used Screened Poisson smoothing to improve the transitions between summits, mountain feet, and plains while retaining the main ridge elevations and the coastline’s zero-elevation boundary.

    I linked the raster conversions and numerical processing for these experiments with Python scripts, making it easier to compare algorithms using the same ridge and coastal controls.

    Generating seafloor terrain and depth contours

    I drew the main mountain ranges on land, while the program generated the seafloor. It first calculated the spherical distance from each offshore location to the coast. Moving outwards, it placed the continental shelf, slope, rise, and deep-ocean basin, so that depth increased continuously from the zero-elevation coastline.

    The program then added ridges, trenches, and relief at different scales, with stronger variation in deeper water. Finally, I combined the negative seafloor elevations and positive land elevations into one global elevation map and extracted contours for both land and ocean.

    Global elevation and contours

    Combining the land and seafloor gave me an 18,000 × 9,000 global elevation map. I extracted land contours and ocean depth contours from this data, then colored the map by elevation to see how the mountains, coasts, and ocean basins worked together across the planet.

    The mountains and ocean basins now had numerical elevations that could be used in simulations. My next question was how winds, temperatures, and rainfall would be distributed over this terrain under the planet’s environmental conditions. I began testing climate models, with the intention of using the resulting rainfall in the erosion experiments that followed.

    4. Climate simulation and terrain adjustments

    With the terrain established, I wanted climate simulations to estimate regional temperatures and precipitation. More importantly, I wanted rainfall to drive the erosion model, so that climate and elevation would jointly determine the rivers and valleys. A simulation starts from specified initial conditions and runs through multiple model years. Only once the long-term trend and fluctuations in global mean temperature have settled is the output ready to be collected for comparison.

    The temperature and precipitation animations show the changes in each simulation run. Even on a T21 grid, every model year takes time to calculate. At higher resolutions, running the model and waiting for its climate to stabilize become much more expensive. I therefore tested parameters on coarser grids before committing to higher-resolution runs.

    Choosing a climate model

    I tried Isca first, but it relies mainly on the CPU. My computer has a relatively strong GPU and more limited CPU performance, so I looked for a model that could use the GPU and tested SpeedyWeather. On my hardware and under my test conditions, it ran roughly ten times faster than Isca.

    However, SpeedyWeather’s precipitation output showed anomalies. The speed advantage was not enough to justify unreliable data, so I switched to the CPU-based ExoPlaSim and began systematically testing my terrain and planetary parameters.

    Running year by year and detecting stability automatically

    Every map revision meant converting the terrain again, configuring the simulation, and waiting for the climate to settle. Doing that by hand became too cumbersome, so I wrote a set of Python scripts on Linux to connect the whole workflow. Terrain preprocessing checks that the map covers the globe, has the correct north–south orientation, and joins continuously across its two edges. My launch script then configures the planetary parameters and runs the calculation across 16 CPU cores.

    At the end of each model year, the script records global mean temperature and compares the recent trend and variability. The climate needs to settle before its results are useful for later analysis. Once those indicators meet my stability conditions for consecutive checks, the script starts collecting the production data and exports it after the specified number of years. I also wrote monitoring scripts for following progress in real time and resuming from a checkpoint after an interruption. BAT launchers let me start the workflow with a single action.

    My post-processing scripts organize annual and monthly results, convert the temperature and precipitation units, and export GeoTIFF files for inspection in geographic software such as QGIS and SAGA GIS. Monthly temperature and precipitation data also let me calculate the climate type that would theoretically occur in each region.

    R02 · Earth-environment baseline

    For the first ExoPlaSim test, I kept Earth’s environmental parameters and replaced only the elevation map with my own. I wanted to establish that the model ran correctly and that its temperature and precipitation over this terrain made sense under standard Earth conditions. I also made animations from 11 consecutive model years to compare changes across the map. This run used a T42 grid.

    My computer is a consumer machine, so very large, high-resolution simulations are difficult to run. Even T42 takes several hours to produce results, and increasing the resolution multiplies that waiting time.

    R03 · Planet size and atmosphere

    Once the Earth baseline worked, I began adding the Star of Embarkation’s own conditions one at a time. Changing everything at once would have made it difficult to identify which setting caused a problem. This run focused on planetary size and atmosphere, adjusting the radius, gravity, and pressure.

    I kept the T42 grid and compared the result with the baseline to see how those changes affected temperature and precipitation.

    R04 · Higher-resolution Earth baseline

    This run retained the Earth reference environment and increased the grid from T42 to T63, giving it 2.25 times as many horizontal grid points.

    T63 was noticeably more expensive to calculate. I compared it with T42 to see how much the higher resolution changed the results, particularly the temperature and precipitation on either side of the mountains, along the coasts, and inland.

    R05 · Starting a finer grid from a coarse-grid result

    A higher-resolution run has to wait for its climate to settle again. I tried a shortcut: run T21 until it was nearly stable, then transfer its final state to T42 and continue from there, hoping to shorten that wait.

    Starting T42 from the lower-resolution T21 result produced abnormal calculations. Temperature and precipitation barely changed afterwards, so I abandoned this way of speeding up the simulation.

    R06 · Coarse-grid parameter preview

    I had not found a reliable shortcut. High-resolution simulations took too long, and renting a computing server was beyond my budget, so I began testing planetary parameters on the smallest grid, T21. I could first see whether the broad temperature and rainfall patterns matched my intentions, then run a complete higher-resolution simulation once the direction looked right.

    This run continued with a 16° axial tilt, smaller than the 23.441° Earth reference used in R03. A smaller tilt reduces seasonal differences in sunlight and temperature, especially at middle and high latitudes. I wanted more gradual seasonal changes in the climate and ecosystems, while also testing the effects of the planet’s rotation and orbital periods.

    R07 · Adjusting stellar irradiance

    Before this run, I had tried reducing stellar irradiance by 5%, but the simulated temperature and precipitation changed dramatically. Irradiance affected the climate much more strongly than I had expected, so I reduced it by only 1% instead. Even that produced noticeable changes. My aim was to ease the heat around the equator of the main continent a little.

    After looking at this run’s temperature and precipitation, I used the data to calculate regional climate types. The classification map below made it easier to see the wet and dry regions, and whether the desert actually appeared where I had intended.

    The classification exposed a problem. I had expected moisture to move from left to right across the map and meet the mountain barrier, leaving a desert in the central lowland behind it. In the simulation, the main moisture transport went the other way: from right to left. The lowland I wanted to be dry became wetter instead. I realized I had misjudged the wind belts and decided to reverse the planet’s rotation to see whether the moisture transport would reverse as well.

    R08 · Testing retrograde rotation

    I first tried representing reverse rotation with a negative rotation period, but the model did not run correctly. After reading the parameter definitions, I used axial tilt instead: below 90°, rotation follows the direction of the orbit; above 90°, it goes in the opposite direction.

    The original tilt was 16°. To keep that amount of inclination while reversing the rotation, I used 180° − 16° = 164°. I then reran the temperature and precipitation simulation at T42, or 128 × 64 grid cells, to examine the new climate distribution.

    R09 · Final high-resolution climate simulation

    The T42 result from R08 was already very close to the climate distribution I had imagined, so I began the final run with more local detail. I increased the grid to T85, or 256 × 128, and ran it continuously for nearly a week to obtain this result.

    The script monitored global mean temperature over the latest 20 model years. It began production sampling only after the stability criteria had been met in five consecutive checks. I collected 105 model years, then organized the data and calculated the mean temperature and precipitation for each month as inputs for the erosion simulation.

    I also used the final monthly temperature and precipitation to produce the corresponding climate classification. R09’s finer grid reveals more local variation and geographic detail than R07.

    The final classification places the dry region I wanted in the center of the continent, with rainforests, wetlands, and different temperature zones elsewhere. This result met my design goals and gave me what I needed for the next step: using its precipitation and runoff to let the erosion model reshape the valleys.

    5. Surface refinement and erosion simulation

    When I had the climate model’s rainfall and was preparing the erosion tests, I found that the terrain was too smooth. Watercourses would line up too regularly, making it difficult to form naturally winding, branching valleys. I therefore added mountain detail before processing the climate model’s low-resolution runoff.

    Refining mountains that were too smooth

    The earlier terrain had undergone substantial interpolation and smoothing to preserve the main ranges and continental outline. Although the mountains had their intended heights, their surfaces were still too smooth. I wrote a QGIS Python tool to add continuous local relief within the existing ranges.

    The added relief varies continuously over distances of tens of kilometers. It becomes stronger at higher elevations and remains gentler in the lowlands, while the coast and seafloor retain their original heights. I then extracted the contours again to see whether the mountain forms had improved.

    The two comparison maps use different elevation palettes; the comparison focuses on changes in mountain detail and relief.

    The revised contours retain the mountains’ overall shape while adding more natural local variation. They also avoid the many deep pits and implausible bumps that direct random perturbations can produce. The result was close to what I had wanted.

    Smoothing the runoff grid

    The final climate simulation uses a 256 × 128 grid, so its precipitation and runoff have that resolution too. Using them directly in a finer erosion model would leave visible square boundaries between cells with different values, causing abrupt changes in erosion depth. I needed to turn that coarse runoff grid into finer data with continuous transitions.

    This is more complicated than enlarging a picture. Climate output contains geographic coordinates and numerical values. Ordinary image smoothing can hide the blocks but may also change the total amount of runoff. I tried interpolation and resampling methods to find a way to smooth the transitions while retaining that total, and eventually found a suitable refinement algorithm.

    I then compared total runoff before and after refinement; the difference was less than 0.2%. The more noticeable local differences were concentrated around the poles. An equirectangular map spreads each pole across an entire row, while meridians meet at the poles on a sphere. Cell areas vary with latitude, making local resampling differences more likely there. Runoff over the main continent was largely unaffected, and this version met the requirements of the erosion simulation.

    Simulating erosion and deposition with goSPL

    I wrote launch and monitoring scripts for goSPL, bringing terrain and climate inputs, spherical mesh resolution, CPU parallelism, and progress monitoring into one workflow. At startup I select the elevation and climate data, then set the mesh and simulation time. After confirmation, I can watch the mesh being built, while a separate monitoring window displays progress through each step. The four screenshots below show these operations in use.

    To let flowing water reshape the terrain, I mapped global elevation and surface runoff onto the same spherical mesh, matching the slope at each location with its water input. I then used goSPL to calculate flow convergence, erosion, sediment transport, and deposition.

    After erosion, rivers previously interrupted by depressions and local rises connect more smoothly, and tributaries join the downstream channels more readily. The drainage network becomes more continuous, with a more coherent relationship between the terrain and its rivers.

    I exported the simulated terrain changes back to geographic rasters and compared them with the original elevations and contours in QGIS. I could see which valleys developed channels and where incision or deposition occurred. I then organized the global drainage directions, river network, and flow accumulation before examining the combined rivers and lakes.

    Erosion and deposition further improved the terrain, wearing down implausible spikes and abrupt protrusions and making the connections between valleys, slopes, and lowlands more natural. Water incision and sediment deposition brought the resulting landforms closer to a plausible pattern of landscape evolution.

    Final hydrology

    The global hydrology map shows the planet’s rivers, lakes, and coastlines. The mainland detail makes it easier to follow the individual channels and lake outlines.

    Once the terrain, climate, and hydrology were organized, I combined them into surface textures and used Blender to examine the entire planet and its ground-level details.

    6. Planet rendering and web presentation

    For a more convincing planetary render, I used unyxium’s Blender scene, Procedural Planet, as a foundation and gradually integrated my own terrain and surface data. The following stages show the development from the first surface material to the complete planet.

    Original model credit

    [1] unyxium. (2020). Procedural Planet [Blender scene: Planet Shader 3.blend]. Blender Artists. Node-group license: CC BY 4.0.

    This project adapts the original scene with custom terrain and surface colors for The Star of Embarkation, retaining the procedural clouds, ocean and atmosphere.

    B01 · Initial surface

    I mapped my global elevation data and surface-color texture onto a sphere. The colors combine elevation, temperature, precipitation, climate types, biomes, and river hydrology to distinguish regions. This first version let me examine the continental proportions, relief, and overall palette.

    B02 · Adding side lighting

    I added side lighting to reveal the slopes and terrain relief, then looked more closely at the valleys and hillsides.

    The differences in mountain height were now visible to the eye, but they still needed to be more pronounced.

    B03 · Surface coordinates and riverbanks

    I increased the terrain’s height variation in this version to make the mountain relief more visible. I fixed the surface texture coordinates, refined the material transitions around ridges, valleys, and riverbanks, and examined the slopes and channels in detail.

    B05 · Integrating the complete planet scene

    I began integrating my terrain and surface textures into unyxium’s complete planetary scene, retaining its ocean, atmosphere, and star field to give the Star of Embarkation the appearance of a complete planet.

    B06 · Adding the cloud texture

    Once the surface looked the way I wanted, I added a cloud texture and adjusted its coverage and lighting to complete the planet’s appearance.

    B07 · Surface saturation and coastal transitions

    In B07, I reduced the surface saturation for a softer overall palette and added stronger irregularity to the coastline, giving bays and headlands more natural outlines. The first two images compare the B05 and B07 coastlines.

    I then refined the transition between nearshore water colors and the independently generated seafloor, making the change from shallow to deep water more natural. The accompanying nearshore view shows the water colors and their connection to the seabed.

    B18–B22 · Refining coastal edges

    B18 adjusted the surface colors using climate data. By B22, I had further refined bays, headlands, and island outlines to give the coastline more detail.

    B28–B32 · Surface and river detail

    In B28, I further refined the islands and coastal terrain while retaining the main continent’s overall structure. By B32, I had increased the detail in the mountains, coasts, and rivers, then examined how those details looked from different distances.

    The scene represents an entire planet, so the rivers still look somewhat coarse when seen close up. Their courses are clearly visible, however, which is enough for this stage of the planetary simulation.

    The channels affected the planet’s overall appearance less than I had expected, but they still provide a geographic basis for my later worldbuilding.

    Interactive web presentation

    The interactive globe uses Three.js. Its surface combines a global UV color texture exported from Blender with normal, roughness, cloud, and atmospheric data. The browser calculates lighting, day and night, and cloud shadows in real time, so the effects remain visible as the planet rotates or the light moves.

    Coastlines, plate boundaries, contours, and climate boundaries use vectors for clear display on the sphere. Climate types can be viewed separately; pointing at a region shows its type and highlights the other areas with the same climate. Rivers and lakes can also be displayed independently or overlaid on other maps.

    Alongside the globe, I made Robinson, equirectangular, and Mollweide map views. Switching views reprojects the same maps and layers, making it easier to examine continental shapes and geographic distributions from different perspectives.

    To keep interaction responsive, the globe loads when it enters the visible area. Once the scene has loaded and the visitor starts interacting, the browser prepares contours and other map data in the background for faster layer changes. It avoids unnecessary redraws while idle and pauses dynamic updates offscreen, reducing work on content that is not currently visible.
