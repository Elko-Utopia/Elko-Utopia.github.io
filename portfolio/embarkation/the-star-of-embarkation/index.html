The Star of Embarkation: Planet and Art

    1. Map requirements and Photoshop sketches

    My goal is to create a planetary map that both serves the story of The Star of Embarkation and follows real-world geographical principles.

    In the story, humans travel from Earth to the Star of Embarkation, where they come into conflict with the Sylphs, an intelligent species native to the planet. The conflict eventually grows into a war between two civilizations. After the war, both humans and Sylphs develop pro-war and anti-war organizations. The map therefore needs geographical buffer zones between different factions, as well as rivers to supply human towns. I also want steep mountains, deserts, forests, lakes, swamps, and other landscapes, so places such as the mountain refuges in the story have a plausible geographical setting.

    The story does not involve naval warfare. For a Sylph civilization that developed on this planet, the existence of several major continents would also significantly affect the direction of the story. I therefore decided to give the planet just one main continent. Control of this continent would give either species an overwhelming geographical advantage, which is why both sides continue to fight over it.

    In my setting, the Star of Embarkation experienced extremely harsh natural conditions in ancient times. To reflect that history, I planned a gigantic meteorite impact crater that would give the main continent its crescent shape.

    While drawing the map in Photoshop, I felt that a planet with only one major landmass looked unrealistic, so I added island groups around it. Many islands are shaped by tectonic activity, so I also drew plate boundaries in the sketch to help determine where the islands would go and to provide a basis for the later terrain design.

    From a flat sketch to a sphere

    After finishing the sketch, I imported the map into GPlates to check the continent’s shape and position on a globe. Because of map projection, the proportions, outlines, and relative positions of the landmasses looked different from those in the flat sketch.

    I therefore used my Photoshop sketch as a starting point and drew vector coastlines and plate boundaries on the sphere in GPlates, creating a new, simplified map outline that better matched the globe’s geometry.

    2. Drawing continents and plates in GPlates

    I set the diameter of the Star of Embarkation at 7,488 miles, slightly smaller than Earth but still on the scale of a terrestrial planet. Combined with gravity slightly lower than Earth’s, this gives characters a wider range of movement. Shorter distances also make it easier to arrange journeys between regions. The main continent covers approximately 13.90 million square miles.

    Naming geographic regions

    Humans bring memories of their old world to the new continent, while the Sylphs name places after water, landforms, and their faith in the Spirit Tree. As a result, the main continent and its mountains have different names in the two cultures. Plate names are used to identify where each plate lies on the planet; the table below lists every plate and its location.

    Once the coastlines, islands, and plates were in place, I needed to turn those geological structures into terrain with real changes in elevation. GPlates is useful for drawing coastlines and plates on a sphere, but I needed to assign an elevation to individual points along mountain ridges, so I moved to QGIS to build the first rough contour-based terrain.

    3. Building initial contour terrain in QGIS

    After drawing the continent, I had to decide where the mountain ranges would run, where they would rise, and how they would descend into lowlands. Drawing every contour line by hand would take an enormous amount of work, and it was hard to predict the elevation boundary of every contour along a long, narrow mountain range.

    Instead, I drew ridge control lines. First, I marked a mountain’s route with a polyline, then placed vertices with specified elevations along it. That way, I only had to decide where the mountain ran and how high those points were. The software could estimate the ground between the ridge elevations and the coastline at zero elevation. The lines defined the direction and height of the mountain ridges, and the first interpolation turned these separate values into a continuous, rough terrain surface.

    Comparing smoothing methods

    While drawing the ridge control lines, I followed the plate layout I had designed earlier. The two plates on the western side of the New Land converge to form the largest and longest mountain range on the Star of Embarkation, which I named the New Andes. The Andes on Earth are among the longest continental mountain ranges, and I wanted to reflect how human settlers would bring familiar names from the old world to similar landscapes in their new home.

    I wanted this mountain range to block moist sea winds moving from west to east, preventing much of that moisture from reaching the interior. Rain would fall on the windward slopes, leaving the land behind the mountains drier and allowing a desert to form in the center of the continent. In the story, that desert would also separate the main areas occupied by humans and Sylphs. I would use the later climate simulations to find out whether this geographical idea worked.

    I first used natural-neighbor interpolation to connect the ridge elevations with the coastline at zero elevation, producing a continuous, rough terrain surface. The image on the left shows the elevation control lines, while the image on the right shows the resulting terrain. Together they show how the position of the mountain ridges affects the slopes.

    Once I had a rough terrain surface, I still needed to smooth out its harsh slopes. I tried TIN interpolation, which preserved the control points but produced obvious triangular facets on the mountainsides. Regularized splines in GRASS GIS made the surface smoother, but the seams left by processing separate regions were also quite visible.

    I eventually used Screened Poisson to adjust the slopes, making the transitions from peaks to foothills and plains more natural while retaining the main ridge elevations and the coastline at zero elevation.

    I connected the raster conversions and numerical processing in these experiments with Python scripts, making it easier to compare different algorithms using the same ridge and coastline control lines.

    Generating seabed terrain and contours

    I drew the main mountain ranges on land, while the program generated the seafloor. I first calculated the distance along the globe from each underwater location to the coastline, then arranged the continental shelf, continental slope, continental rise, and deep-ocean basins from near to far offshore, so the seabed descended continuously from the shoreline at zero elevation.

    The program then added mid-ocean ridges, trenches, and variations at different scales to this continuous seabed. The variations gradually grew stronger towards the deep ocean. Finally, I combined the negative elevations of the seafloor with the positive elevations of the land into one global elevation map and used the program to extract contour lines, including underwater depth contours.

    Global elevation and contours

    After combining land and seabed, I obtained a global elevation map measuring 18,000 × 9,000 pixels. I extracted land contours and underwater depth contours from the same elevation data, then colored the map by elevation to check whether the planet’s mountains, coasts, and ocean basins worked together.

    At this stage, the mountains and ocean basins had numerical elevations that could be used directly in later simulations. I wanted to find out how wind, temperature, and precipitation would be distributed across this terrain under the planet’s environmental conditions. I therefore moved on to climate simulation, with the aim of using its rainfall data for the erosion tests that followed.

    4. Climate simulation and terrain adjustments

    With the terrain established, I wanted to use climate simulations to calculate the temperature and precipitation in different regions. More importantly, I wanted to feed that precipitation into the later erosion model so that the locations of rivers and valleys would be shaped by both climate and elevation, in keeping with geographical and climatic processes. Each simulation begins with a set of initial conditions and runs through successive model years. I collect data for comparison only after the long-term trend and fluctuations in global average temperature have gradually stabilized.

    The temperature and precipitation animations show the changes across different simulation runs. Even on a T21 grid, each model year takes time to calculate; increasing the resolution makes it more expensive to run through many years and wait for the climate to stabilize. I therefore tested the parameters on a coarser grid before running high-resolution simulations.

    Climate model selection

    I first tried Isca, which relies mainly on the CPU. My computer had a relatively powerful GPU but a more limited CPU, so I looked for a climate model that could use the GPU and tested SpeedyWeather. Under my own hardware and test conditions, it calculated at about ten times the speed of Isca.

    However, SpeedyWeather produced abnormal precipitation output, and its speed advantage could not make up for unreliable data. I eventually switched to the CPU-based ExoPlaSim to systematically test my hand-drawn terrain and the Star of Embarkation’s parameters.

    Year-by-year runs and automatic stability checks

    Every time I changed the map, I had to convert the terrain again, configure the simulation environment, and wait for the climate to stabilize. Doing all of this manually was tedious, so I wrote a set of Python scripts on Linux to connect the steps into one workflow. Terrain preprocessing first checks whether the map covers the entire globe, whether north and south are correctly oriented, and whether the seam between the two ends joins continuously. My launcher then configures the planetary parameters automatically and uses 16 CPU cores in parallel to shorten the calculation time.

    At the end of each model year, my scripts record the global average temperature and compare its trend and fluctuations over recent years. The results become suitable for further analysis only as the temperature changes stabilize. Once those measurements satisfy my stability criteria for several consecutive checks, the scripts start collecting data and export it after the specified number of years. I also wrote a monitoring script to view progress in real time and resume from checkpoints after an interruption. A BAT launcher starts the whole process with one click.

    I also wrote post-processing scripts to organize annual and monthly simulation results, convert temperature and precipitation units, and export GeoTIFF files that I can inspect in QGIS, SAGA GIS, and other geographic software. Using the monthly temperature and precipitation data, I can also estimate each region’s climate classification.

    R02 · Earth-reference test

    For my first ExoPlaSim run, I kept the Earth-reference environmental settings and replaced only the terrain with my own elevation map. I wanted to check whether the model would run correctly and whether this terrain produced reasonable temperature and precipitation under standard Earth conditions. I also turned the results from 11 consecutive model years into temperature and precipitation animations to compare regional changes. This run used a T42 grid.

    R03 · Planetary size and atmosphere

    After the Earth-reference run worked, I began introducing the Star of Embarkation’s own planetary conditions one at a time. Changing every parameter at once would make it difficult to identify which setting caused a problem. In this run, I started with the planet’s size and atmosphere, adjusting the radius, gravity, and air pressure.

    I kept the T42 grid and compared the results with the first run to see how these changes affected temperature and precipitation.

    R04 · Higher-resolution Earth reference

    This run kept the Earth-reference environment and increased the grid from T42 to T63, giving it 2.25 times as many horizontal grid points.

    T63 required substantially more computing time. I compared its results with T42 to see whether the higher resolution changed the simulation, focusing on temperature and precipitation on either side of the mountain ranges, along the coasts, and in the interior.

    R05 · Low-to-high resolution restart test

    Higher-resolution simulations had to go through climate stabilization all over again, so I tried a shortcut. I ran T21 until it was nearly stable, then transferred its final state to T42 in the hope of cutting down the waiting time.

    But when I tried to start the higher-resolution T42 model using results from the lower-resolution T21 run, the calculations became abnormal, with temperature and precipitation barely changing. I therefore abandoned this faster approach.

    R06 · Lower-resolution parameter preview

    I could not find a reliable way to speed up the runs, high-resolution simulations took too long, and renting a computing server was beyond my budget. I therefore started testing planetary settings on the smallest T21 grid. Once the broad temperature and precipitation patterns looked right, I could run the complete simulation at a higher resolution.

    This run kept the 16° axial tilt, lower than the Earth-reference value of 23.441° used in R03. A smaller tilt generally means milder seasonal changes in sunlight and temperature, particularly at middle and high latitudes. I hoped this would also make seasonal changes in regional climates and ecosystems less pronounced, while testing the effects of the planet’s rotation and orbital periods.

    R07 · Irradiation adjustment

    Before this run, I had tried reducing stellar irradiance by 5%, but the simulated temperature and precipitation changed dramatically. That showed me how much more sensitive the climate was to irradiance than I had expected, so I reduced it by just 1% instead. Even this smaller adjustment produced noticeable changes, and I hoped it would ease the heat near the equator on the main continent.

    After examining the temperature and precipitation changes from this run, I used those data to calculate the climate types in different regions. The classification map below made it easier to see which places were wet, which were dry, and whether the desert had formed where I planned it.

    But the classification map revealed a problem. I had expected moisture near the main continent to move mainly from left to right across the map, get blocked by the mountain range, and leave the central lowland behind it dry enough for a desert. The simulation showed the opposite prevailing direction: moisture moved from right to left, and the lowland I wanted to be dry became wetter. I realized I had misjudged the direction of the prevailing winds, so I decided to try reversing the planet’s rotation to see whether the moisture flow would reverse too.

    R08 · Retrograde rotation test

    To test reverse rotation, I initially tried setting a negative rotation period, but the model failed to run. After checking the parameter definitions, I used axial tilt to specify retrograde rotation: an angle below 90° indicates rotation in the same direction as the orbit, while an angle above 90° indicates the opposite direction.

    The original axial tilt was 16°. I wanted to keep the same degree of tilt while reversing the rotation, so I used 180° − 16° = 164°. I then ran T42 (128 × 64) again to see how temperature, precipitation, and the overall climate distribution changed.

    R09 · Final high-resolution climate simulation

    The T42 results from R08 came very close to the climate distribution I had originally intended, so I began building the final version with more detailed climate data. I increased the grid to T85 (256 × 128) and ran the simulation almost continuously for a week to produce these results.

    This time, my scripts examined the global mean temperature over the latest 20 model years, starting formal data collection only after the stability criteria were met and five consecutive checks passed. I collected 105 model years of data, then calculated the monthly average temperature and precipitation to prepare inputs for the later erosion simulation.

    After obtaining the final precipitation results, I also used the monthly temperature and precipitation data to make a corresponding climate classification map. The finer grid in R09 captured more local differences and geographic detail than the earlier R07 run.

    5. Surface refinement and erosion modeling

    When I obtained the precipitation data from the climate simulation and was ready to test erosion, I noticed that the elevation terrain I had made earlier was too smooth. This would make the rivers form overly regular patterns, making naturally winding and branching valleys difficult to create. I therefore added detail to the mountains first, then refined the low-resolution runoff data from the climate model.

    Refining overly smooth terrain

    Earlier, I had applied strong interpolation and smoothing to preserve the main mountain ranges and continental outline. The elevations were already determined, but many mountain surfaces were still too smooth. I therefore wrote a QGIS Python tool to add continuous rises and dips within the existing ranges.

    These variations extend continuously over dozens of miles, with more pronounced changes at higher elevations and smaller ones in lowlands. I kept the coastline and seabed at their original elevations, then extracted the contours again to check whether the mountain shapes had improved.

    The two comparison images use different colors to represent elevation. I focused on changes in mountain detail and relief.

    The updated contours retained the overall shape of the original mountains while adding more natural local variations. They also avoided the many deep pits and unnatural bumps that direct random perturbations can produce, bringing the terrain close to the result I wanted.

    Smoothing the runoff grid

    The final climate simulation used a 256 × 128 grid, so its precipitation and runoff data had the same resolution. Applying these values directly to a much finer erosion model would leave visible square boundaries between neighboring cells. Erosion depth could change abruptly along those pixel edges. I therefore needed to turn the coarse runoff data into a smoother, higher-resolution field.

    This was more complicated than enlarging an ordinary image. The climate output is geospatial data, with real numerical values tied to latitude and longitude. Ordinary image smoothing can hide the blocks, but it can also change the total runoff. I tried numerical interpolation and regridding approaches to find a way to smooth the transitions while preserving the overall water total, eventually arriving at a suitable method.

    I then compared the total runoff before and after refinement and found a difference of less than 0.2%. The more noticeable local differences were near the poles. On a rectangular latitude-longitude map, polar regions stretch across entire rows, although the meridians converge on the sphere and the actual area represented by each cell changes with latitude. This makes local regridding differences more pronounced there. The runoff pattern across the main continent was largely unaffected, so this version met the needs of the erosion simulation.

    Calculating erosion and deposition with goSPL

    I wrote launch and monitoring scripts for goSPL, bringing the selection of terrain and climate data, spherical mesh resolution, CPU parallel settings, and progress monitoring into one workflow. First, I choose an elevation map and climate dataset, then set the mesh resolution and simulation duration. Once I confirm the settings, I can watch the mesh being built; a separate monitor shows each step of the run. The four screenshots below show these actual operations in order.

    I mapped the global elevation and surface runoff onto the same spherical mesh so that each location had both a terrain slope and a corresponding water input. I then used goSPL to calculate drainage directions and the erosion, sediment transport, and deposition caused by flowing water.

    After erosion, rivers previously interrupted by depressions and local rises connect more smoothly, and tributaries join the downstream channels more readily. The drainage network becomes more continuous, with a more coherent relationship between the terrain and its rivers.

    I exported the simulated terrain changes as geospatial rasters and compared them with the original elevation map and contours in QGIS. This let me check where channels formed along valleys, where they cut into the ground, and where sediment accumulated. I then produced maps of global drainage directions, river networks, and flow accumulation before examining the final rivers and lakes.

    The erosion and deposition simulation also corrected the terrain itself. It wore down implausible sharp peaks and abrupt bumps, producing more natural transitions between river valleys, slopes, and lowlands. The final terrain better reflects the way landscapes develop through flowing water and sediment deposition.

    Final hydrology

    The final hydrology results show the rivers, lakes, and coastlines across the entire planet. A close view of the main continent shows the specific paths of its channels and lakes.

    Once I had organized the terrain, climate, and hydrology results, I combined them into surface textures and examined the whole planet and local terrain details in Blender.

    6. Planet renders and the web presentation

    For a more convincing planetary render, I used unyxium’s Blender scene, Procedural Planet, as a foundation, then gradually brought my own simulated terrain and surface data into it. The following stages show how the surface evolved into the complete planet.

    Original model credit

    [1] unyxium. (2020). Procedural Planet [Blender scene: Planet Shader 3.blend]. Blender Artists. Node-group license: CC BY 4.0.

    This project adapts the original scene with custom terrain and surface colors for The Star of Embarkation, retaining the procedural clouds, ocean and atmosphere.

    B01 · Initial surface

    I mapped my global elevation data and surface-color texture onto a sphere. The surface colors combine elevation, temperature, precipitation, climate zones, biomes, and river hydrology to distinguish different regions. This first version let me check continental proportions, relief, and the overall color scheme.

    B02 · Adding side lighting

    I added side lighting to bring out the slopes and terrain relief, then examined valleys and hillsides up close.

    At this point, the mountain relief was visible to the naked eye, but it was still fairly subtle.

    B03 · Surface coordinates and riverbanks

    To make the mountain relief more visible, I increased the terrain’s height variation in this version. I fixed the surface texture coordinates, refined the material transitions around ridges, valleys, and riverbanks, and checked the details of the slopes and river channels.

    B05 · Integrating the complete planet scene

    I began integrating my terrain and surface textures into unyxium’s complete planetary scene, retaining its ocean, atmosphere, and star field to present the Star of Embarkation as a fully rendered planet.

    B06 · Adding the cloud texture

    Once I was satisfied with the surface, I added a cloud texture and adjusted its coverage and lighting to complete the planet’s appearance.

    B07 · Surface saturation and coastal transitions

    In B07, I reduced the saturation of the surface colors for a softer overall palette and introduced stronger irregularity along the coastline, making bays and headlands look more natural. The first two images compare the coast in B05 and B07.

    I then refined the transition between nearshore water colors and the independently generated seafloor. The third image shows the nearshore water and seabed transition, while the fourth shows the adjusted planet as a whole.

    B18–B22 · Refining coastlines

    B18 adjusted the surface colors using the climate data. By B22, I had further refined the outlines of bays, headlands, and islands, adding more detail to the coastline.

    B28–B32 · Surface and river details

    In B28, I further refined the islands and coastal terrain while preserving the overall shape of the main continent. By B32, I had added more detail to mountains, coasts, and rivers, and checked how these features looked at different viewing distances.

    Because the render covers the entire planet, the local river details still look somewhat coarse. Even so, the river courses are clearly visible, which is sufficient for this stage of the planetary simulation.

    The river channels had less impact on the planet’s overall appearance than I had expected, but they will still provide a geographic foundation for my later worldbuilding.

    Interactive web presentation

    The interactive planet on the website uses Three.js. Its surface uses the global UV color texture exported from Blender, together with normal, roughness, cloud, and atmosphere data. The browser calculates lighting, day–night shading, and cloud shadows in real time, so these effects remain visible as I rotate the planet or move the light.

    Coastlines, plate boundaries, contour lines, and climate-zone boundaries all use vector data for clear display on the globe. Climate zones can also be viewed on their own; pointing at a region shows its climate type and highlights matching climate regions. Rivers and lakes can likewise be shown as a separate layer or overlaid on other maps.

    Alongside the 3D globe, I created a Robinson projection, a flat world map, and a Mollweide projection. Switching projections displays the same map data and layers in the chosen projection, making it easier to compare continental shapes and geographic patterns.

    To keep the website responsive, the planet loads only when it enters the visible area. Once the current scene has loaded and the user starts interacting, the browser prepares contour lines and other map-layer data in the background so that later switches are faster. It avoids unnecessary redraws while idle and pauses animation updates when the viewer is offscreen, instead of spending resources on content that is not currently visible.
